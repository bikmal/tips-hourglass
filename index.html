Where the gradient comes from

At TIPS flow rates, most of the added gradient comes from blood decelerating out of the waist, not from friction along it. In this model, waist diameter and flare shape drive the gradient more than waist length does.
Model and assumptions
  • The portal circulation is two resistances in parallel: the liver and the shunt. Portal inflow is held constant, so the predicted gradient rise is an upper estimate; in reality splanchnic inflow may fall as the gradient rises.
  • Liver resistance is calibrated from the pre-TIPS gradient (Rliver = PSGpre-TIPS / Q), ignoring spontaneous collaterals.
  • Everything else in the shunt pathway (stent ends, hepatic vein, angulation) is lumped into one resistance calibrated from the measured gradient before reduction.
  • Added waist losses: Poiseuille friction integrated along the stent profile, ΔP = Q·∫ 8μ/(πr⁴) dx, plus a Borda–Carnot exit loss, ΔP = K·ρ/2·(Uwaist − Ustent)², with diffuser coefficient K = min(1, 2.6·sin θ) for flare half-angle θ.
  • Steady, Newtonian flow in rigid tubes; blood viscosity 3.5 mPa·s, density 1060 kg/m³. Waist Reynolds numbers above about 2000 suggest turbulence, which this model does not capture, so losses may be underestimated.
  • The flow animation is slowed and schematic: particles follow the stent contour with a parabolic profile. Recirculation shading marks where flow separation is expected (flare half-angle above about 7°), not a computed flow field.
